# Rebecca 🔬 — canon: research and source evaluation

> The portable canon of the craft (#4516), read on demand at the start of a research task, never
> always-on. General professional knowledge only. Lookup order: this page, then the repo's source
> store where the lens names one, then the web.

**Grades.** **A**: a consensus standard, a guideline, or a systematic review or meta-analysis.
**B**: a landmark primary study or a widely used professional handbook. **C**: influential, but
contested, limited to one population, or expert opinion.

| Source | Key finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Guyatt et al. (2008), *GRADE: an emerging consensus on rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations*, BMJ | Certainty of evidence is rated high, moderate, low or very low; study design sets the start, and risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias move it down. | A |
| Higgins et al. (eds.), *Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions*, version 6 | The method standard for a systematic review: a protocol up front, a reproducible search, risk-of-bias assessment per study. | A |
| Page et al. (2021), *The PRISMA 2020 statement*, BMJ | What a review must report so a reader can judge it: search strategy, selection flow, exclusions with reasons. | A |
| Ioannidis (2005), *Why most published research findings are false*, PLoS Medicine | Small studies, small effects, many tested relations and flexible designs lower the chance that a positive finding is true. | B |
| Open Science Collaboration (2015), *Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science*, Science | About a third of 100 replications reached significance, with effects roughly half the original size: a single published study is a lead, not a fact. | B |
| Wineburg & McGrew (2019), *Lateral reading and the nature of expertise*, Teachers College Record | Professional fact-checkers judge a source by leaving it and reading what others say about it, and beat experts who read the page itself. | B |
| Booth, Colomb, Williams et al., *The Craft of Research* (4th ed., 2016) | A research report turns a topic into a question, a question into a problem someone cares about, and answers it with claim, reasons and evidence. | C |
